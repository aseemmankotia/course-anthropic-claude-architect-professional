**Q1.** On exam day inside the OnVUE interface, a question displays square checkboxes next to each option instead of round radio buttons. What does this indicate?

A) The question allows partial credit for selecting any correct option
B) The interface has a rendering glitch and should be reported immediately
C) The question is a multiple-response item requiring more than one selection
D) The question requires selecting options in a specific order

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: C**

OnVUE renders multiple-response questions with checkboxes and single-answer questions with round radio buttons. Checkboxes are the visual cue that more than one answer is expected, and the prompt text always states exactly how many to select.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Multiple-response scoring is all-or-nothing, regardless of input style — there is no partial credit.
- Checkbox rendering is standard OnVUE behavior for multi-response items, not a glitch.
- Selection order never matters — only the final selected set is scored.

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q2.** A candidate reaches question 32 of 63 with 58 minutes remaining on a 120-minute exam. Based on standard pacing math (~114 seconds per question), what should the candidate do?

A) Continue at current pace since they are roughly on schedule
B) Slow down immediately since they are far ahead of pace
C) Speed up significantly because they are behind pace
D) Skip all remaining questions and guess to save time

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: A**

31 completed questions at a 114-second budget equal ~58.9 minutes of ideal elapsed time; the candidate has used ~61 minutes — only about 2-3 minutes behind, which is within normal pacing variance.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- A 2-3 minute deviation does not mean the candidate is ahead of pace.
- This small deviation across 31 questions is not a serious pacing crisis requiring major speed-up.
- Guessing on remaining questions abandons scoring opportunity and is not a pacing fix.

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q3.** Which flagging strategy is MOST effective for the 63-question, 120-minute exam format?

A) Flag every question for review regardless of confidence, then review all 63 at the end
B) Never flag questions and move forward only, submitting immediately after question 63
C) Flag questions you are confident about to confirm answers at the end
D) Flag only questions with genuine uncertainty, answer with best guess, then use remaining time to revisit flagged items first

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

Flagging only uncertain items preserves limited review time for questions most likely to change the outcome, while every question still has an answer recorded in case time runs out.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Flagging all 63 questions wastes review time sorting confident answers from uncertain ones.
- Never flagging removes the ability to prioritize review time on the hardest items.
- Reviewing confident answers wastes time on items least likely to need correction.

</details>

**Q5.** A candidate scores 740 on the CCAR-P exam, which is scaled from 100-1000 with a passing threshold of 720. What does this scaled score represent?

A) The candidate answered exactly 74% of all 63 questions correctly
B) The candidate answered 74 out of 100 scenario questions correctly
C) The candidate's raw performance converted to a standardized scale that accounts for item difficulty, not a direct raw percentage
D) The candidate needs a minimum raw score of 740 points out of 1000 total available points

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: C**

Scaled scores normalize raw performance to adjust for differences in item difficulty across exam forms; they are not a direct percentage of correct answers.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Scaled scores do not map one-to-one with raw percentage correct.
- The exam has 63 total questions, not 100, so this percentage framing is incorrect.
- There is no raw 1000-point total — 1000 is the ceiling of the scaled range, not an achievable raw point count.

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q6.** With 10 minutes remaining and 5 flagged questions spanning two domains, one weighted 30% and one weighted 10% of the exam, which review order maximizes expected score impact?

A) Review questions from the 10%-weighted domain first since they are likely easier
B) Review questions from the 30%-weighted domain first since errors there carry more scoring impact
C) Review questions in the order they were flagged regardless of domain
D) Skip review entirely and leave original answers unchanged

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

Domain weight determines scoring impact; correcting errors in a heavier-weighted domain has a larger effect on the final scaled score.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Perceived ease has no bearing on scoring weight; prioritizing the lighter domain wastes limited time.
- Flagging order is arbitrary and unrelated to which domain affects the scaled score most.
- With flagged uncertainty and time remaining, skipping review forfeits a chance to correct errors.

</details>

**Q7.** A multiple-response question instructs the candidate to select the two correct options. The candidate selects one correct option and one incorrect option. What score does the candidate receive for this question?

A) Zero credit, since the full correct set was not selected
B) Half credit for the one correct selection
C) Full credit, since at least one selection was correct
D) The question is excluded from scoring due to partial selection

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: A**

Multiple-response questions are scored all-or-nothing; the exact correct set must be selected to receive any credit.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- There is no partial credit mechanism for multi-response items.
- One correct selection within an incorrect combination does not earn full credit.
- Partially answered multi-response questions are still scored, and are marked incorrect.

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q8.** Why does the CCAR-P exam report a scaled score (100-1000) instead of a raw percentage of questions answered correctly?

A) To make the exam appear more difficult than it actually is
B) To allow fair comparison across different exam forms that may vary slightly in item difficulty
C) Because the exam does not track which individual questions were answered correctly
D) To round all scores up to the nearest hundred for simplicity

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

Scaled scoring is a psychometric equating method that allows results from different exam forms with slightly varying item difficulty to be compared fairly.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Scaling is a fairness mechanism, not a tool to inflate perceived difficulty.
- Item-level correctness is tracked and forms the basis for the scaling calculation.
- Scaled scores are not simple rounded raw percentages — they result from statistical equating.

</details>
